## Two phases.
Which area must balance? — Maxwell’s rule on a van der Waals isotherm. [en]

Source: https://koyamahr.github.io/the-art-of-thermal-instability/essays/equal-area.html
Coverage: reading

For molar volume v, the van der Waals equation is p(T,v) = RT/(v − b) − a/v². The attraction term and excluded volume can give a loop below the critical temperature. At a fixed temperature, Maxwell’s construction selects the liquid–vapour coexistence pressure by balancing signed areas on the pressure–volume diagram:
p(T, v_liquid) = p(T, v_vapour) = p_coexist
∫[v_liquid → v_vapour] [p(T,v) − p_coexist] dv = 0
In modern thermodynamic terms, the two phases share temperature, pressure and chemical potential. The horizontal segment represents changing phase fractions. The mechanically unstable portion of the homogeneous loop is bypassed. Equal areas are coordinate dependent: replacing volume by density requires the inverse-square Jacobian; equal-looking areas on a pressure–density plot do not give the same rule.
Maxwell’s 1875 paper explicitly describes a horizontal line cutting equal areas from an isothermal curve, using a reversible-cycle work argument. The transcription of the original Chemical Society paper preserves that argument. The construction is properly called Maxwell’s equal-area rule, applied here to a van der Waals equation of state.

## Two phases.
Which area must balance? — ZP saturation balances a different integral. [en]

Source: https://koyamahr.github.io/the-art-of-thermal-instability/essays/equal-area.html
Coverage: reading

The cold and warm interstellar phases have different temperatures and are maintained by external heating and radiative cooling. With net volumetric cooling Q = n²Λ − nΓ, a static planar conductive front satisfies:
F = κ(T) dT/dx
F dF/dT = κ(T) Q(T,P)
F(T_cold) = F(T_warm) = 0
∫[T_cold → T_warm] κ(T) Q(T,P_sat) dT = 0
This derivation assumes a static planar front with no mass flux and the specified local cooling law and conductivity. It is an energy-transport selection condition. Thermodynamic liquid–vapour coexistence does not directly supply this pressure. The analogy is cancellation of signed integrals; the ZP condition includes a conductivity weight and integrates over temperature. A Maxwell construction on the interstellar pressure–density equilibrium curve would generally select a different value.
For the corrected KI2002 fit with κ = 2500√T, our computed benchmark gives Psat/kB = 2822.884297 K cm⁻³, with cold and warm endpoints at 54.980188 K and 6417.132178 K. No molecular van der Waals force is added to that gas model.

## Two phases.
Which area must balance? — A scalar Ginzburg–Landau connection. [en]

Source: https://koyamahr.github.io/the-art-of-thermal-instability/essays/equal-area.html
Coverage: reading

A bistable reaction–diffusion equation can have the structure of real, overdamped time-dependent Ginzburg–Landau relaxation. Two stable thermal phases correspond to two potential minima; the unstable equilibrium lies between them. An interface is a domain wall. Equal well depths permit a static planar wall; unequal depths drive its motion.
Elphick, Regev & Shaviv (1992), Dynamics of Fronts in Thermally Bistable Fluids, ApJ 392, 106–117, provide a direct precedent. Their isobaric one-dimensional reduction uses a Lagrangian mass coordinate m. Equations (2.14) and (3.2)–(3.3) have the following dimensionless gradient-flow form:
Z_t = F(Z) + Z^β Z_mm = −Z^β δE/δZ
G(Z,p) = Z^(−β) F(Z,p) = −∂U/∂Z
E[Z] = ∫ [½ (∂Z/∂m)² + U(Z,p)] dm
Z = T^α, β = (α − 1)/α, κ ∝ T^α
For positive temperature the mobility Z^β is positive. With vanishing boundary terms, dE/dt = −∫ Z^(−β) (Z_t)² dm ≤ 0. Their Figure 1 and page 109 explicitly connect equal potential minima with a stationary ZP kink. This source includes fluid motion within the isobaric reduction; it does not reduce the entire compressible problem to an arbitrary scalar equation.

The following change of variable is our derivation from the static front equation, rather than a claim that the paper uses the same variable or potential normalization:
θ(T) = ∫ κ(T) dT
θ_xx = Q(T(θ), P)
V(θ;P) = ∫ Q(T(θ), P) dθ
V(θ_warm;P) − V(θ_cold;P)
 = ∫[T_cold → T_warm] κ(T) Q(T,P) dT
At Psat the last integral vanishes: the two minima have equal depths. At a uniform equilibrium, V″ = (∂Q/∂T)P / κ, so the isobarically stable branches are potential minima. For a formal scalar relaxation θ_t = M(θ)[θ_xx − ∂V/∂θ] with M > 0, the same static equation is stationary. This construction establishes the static mathematical correspondence; it does not derive the full time-dependent gas dynamics.
A symmetric quartic double well is a useful cubic-reaction illustration. The KI cooling law is not exactly cubic, and its equal-depth wells need not be mirror symmetric. The functional above is an effective Lyapunov functional of a reduced, externally heated and radiatively cooled system; it is not identified with the equilibrium thermodynamic free energy of the gas. The analogy concerns a real scalar relaxation model, not the full complex order parameter and electromagnetic structure of superconducting Ginzburg–Landau theory. Temperature is not a conserved order parameter, while the underlying gas still conserves mass.

## How thermal conduction
sets a small-scale limit. — One cubic, one growing root. [en]

Source: https://koyamahr.github.io/the-art-of-thermal-instability/field-1965/
Coverage: reading

Field writes perturbations as exp(nt + i k·r): positive real n means growth. Using equations (27) and (38), define q = k/kρ, x = n/(kρc), α = kT/kρ, and β = kρ/kK. Here c is the adiabatic sound speed and the three characteristic wavenumbers are defined by equation (16).
x³ + (α + βq²)x² + q²x + q²(α − 1 + βq²)/γ = 0
Equation (39), regrouped as a cubic in x. The heat-loss function is energy losses minus gains per unit mass per unit time. β = 0 is the zero-conductivity limit. These normalizations assume kρ > 0, as in Figure 2.
For these parameters the polynomial is strictly increasing for nonnegative x. Within the unstable band it has exactly one positive root, which the code finds by bisection. At q = 0 it uses the limiting value x = 0.
For β > 0, the neutral cutoff is qc = √[(1 − α)/β]: approximately 7.0711, 2.2361, and 0.7071 for β = 0.01, 0.1, and 1. The first lies just beyond the plotted range, as in the original figure. Stable-domain growth values are left blank in the CSV.

Source: https://koyamahr.github.io/the-art-of-thermal-instability/research-paths.html
Coverage: reading

Reproducing a large multidimensional simulation can require substantial computing time, storage, and access to original inputs. Our modern-paper exhibits can instead provide a source guide, documented setup, analysis of available outputs, or a small benchmark that isolates a physical mechanism.
Source and method: link the paper and any public code, inputs, and datasets; record what is actually available.
Reuse and analysis: regenerate a diagnostic from released outputs where possible, preserving units, provenance, and processing steps.
Small calculations: evaluate an analytic relation, a one-zone chemical model, a front, or a convergence test within an explicit reduced scope.
Optional full runs: record the resolution, solver, hardware, wall time, and storage needed. A full 2D/3D rerun is not a condition for completing a source or analysis exhibit.
For new nonlinear thermal-instability simulation exhibits, the thermal-conduction treatment and resolution of its relevant scales are selection criteria to audit explicitly. Analytical theory and microphysical rate references are documented separately. Digitized published curves will be identified as digitizations. Reduced models will state their omissions; their results will not be labeled as full-simulation reproductions.

## From two phases
to chemistry and cloud formation. — Global Effects of Thermal Conduction on Two-Phase Media [en]

Source: https://koyamahr.github.io/the-art-of-thermal-instability/research-paths.html
Coverage: reading

Convection and radiative condensation are distinct: Chandrasekhar’s textbook is included as a foundational monograph, not a journal paper. Its proposed benchmark is buoyancy-driven convection of a layer heated from below. Rayleigh-number onset must not be identified with the cooling-derivative criterion for the Field condensation mode, which does not require gravity.
The Boussinesq comparison retains velocity, a pressure constraint and buoyancy while filtering acoustic compressibility. It does not mean zero flow or zero pressure gradient. The resulting hierarchy for the proposed explanation is: selected reaction–diffusion models → Boussinesq fluid dynamics → compressible thermal gas. These are different approximations and drivers, not a single universal equation.
Spiegel & Veronis (1960), On the Boussinesq Approximation for a Compressible Fluid, gives a primary-source account of its assumptions: a thin layer relative to scale heights and suitably small fluctuations. Density variations remain in the buoyancy force. The compressible-fluid reduction also requires care with the adiabatic temperature gradient.
The textbook’s bibliography is checked; its exact equations, sections and figures are not yet audited because full-text access attempts failed. No textbook figure is claimed reproduced. Source review and proposed scope / JSON.

Periodic 4.8 × 3.0 pc box, 256 × 160 cells (Δx = Δy = 0.01875 pc). The compressible Euler equations include momentum, advection, compressional work, thermal conduction and net radiative cooling. There is no forcing, magnetic field, gravity or evolving chemistry.
γ = 5/3; m = 1.37 mH; n = ρ/m; P = nkBT. Mean density is 2.8 cm⁻³, initially at unstable thermal equilibrium. A seeded low-wave-number density perturbation has 10% RMS; temperature adjusts to keep initial pressure uniform and velocity starts at zero.
Γ = 2 × 10⁻²⁶ erg s⁻¹ per particle; Q = n²Λ(T) − nΓ. We use the corrected KI-type analytic cooling fit, evaluating its density argument as the explicitly defined n above. This is a phenomenological model with no separate ionization solution.
κ(T) = 3 × 10⁶ + 3 × 10⁸(100 K / T)² erg cm⁻¹ s⁻¹ K⁻¹. This enlarged, cold-enhanced coefficient broadens thermal interfaces for a modest grid. It differs from the Parker law and the conductivity in the KI equilibrium benchmark. Its own ZP saturation pressure and equilibrium intersections are recorded in diagnostics.json; those values must not replace the existing KI/Parker result.
